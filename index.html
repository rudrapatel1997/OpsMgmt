Course overview

Operations is the set of processes by which an organization turns inputs (labor, material, knowledge, equipment) into outputs (goods and services). This course studies how business strategy demands specific operational capabilities, and how operational capabilities in turn enable or constrain strategy — then gets tactical: capacity, throughput, inventory, flow time, quality, and variability, from a single process up to a full supply chain.

By the end of the course, you should be able to…

  • 01Define and relate the fundamental process measures: throughput, capacity, inventory, and flow time.
  • 02Diagnose system performance and identify the main causes of distress in a process.
  • 03Measure variability, and calculate flow times under demand and supply variability.
  • 04Determine appropriate means to expand capacity, and set inventory policy parameters (order quantities, reorder points).
  • 05Explain systemic variability in supply chains (the bullwhip effect) and how to counter it.
  • 06Identify a firm's required vs. actual process capabilities, and recommend process changes to close that gap.

Case file: Chef Davide Oldani & Ristorante D’O HBS 613-080 · set July 2012

The scene
A sweltering July afternoon, 2012. Oldani preps for another sold-out night at his one-Michelin-star restaurant outside Milan — 35 seats, an 18-month waiting list, and years since a seat sat empty at lunch or dinner. He's weighing how to "spread the POP concept": clone D’O abroad, or open a bigger, cheaper sibling next door.

Oldani & D’O — background

  • •Born 1967, Milan. A serious football injury at 16 ended a promising playing career and redirected him fully into cooking.
  • •Trained under three-Michelin-star Chef Gualtiero Marchesi twice: as a two-month teenage apprentice, then from 1986 as commis, reaching chef de partie (1988) and finally sous-chef (1992–1994/96).
  • •Also worked under Albert Roux (Le Gavroche, London), Alain Ducasse (Louis XV, Monaco), and Pierre Hermé (Fauchon, Paris) — 1989 onward.
  • •Ran the 300-cover Giannino restaurant in Milan 1996–2000; earned it a Michelin star after 18 months.
  • •2000–2003: independent consultant (Master Foods, Hotel George V) before opening his own place.
  • •Opened D’O Oct 23, 2003 in his hometown Cornaredo (35 min from Milan) with a staff of 4 (himself, a sous-chef, a commis, a sommelier). First Michelin star arrived within a year (2004).

The POP concept & how D’O actually runs

  • •POP = "popular" — Michelin-star quality at trattoria prices, deliberately reaching customers who'd never normally visit a starred restaurant.
  • •200 sqm, two dining rooms (25 + 10 seats = 35 total). Open 5 days/week, closed Sunday & Monday (most Michelin restaurants instead close Sat/Sun).
  • •Three dinner time slots (7:30 / 8:30 / 9:30pm); ~half of bookings land on the 8:30 slot. A 7:30 table can be rebooked for 9:30 (two turns); an 8:30 booking blocks the table for the night.
  • •Lunch: only the 25-seat room opens, typically one turn; lunch reservations run ~6 weeks out, versus an 18-month wait overall.
  • •Average total dining time: 90 minutes. Cash only (avoids 3–6% card fees); meticulous tax bookkeeping ("every euro is accounted for").
  • •No cover charge, no service charge. Four seasonal menus/year, ~18 dishes each (4 apps, 8–10 mains, 4 desserts). Signature dish: caramelized onion. Historically 80–90% new dishes each season, now trending toward 40–60% new / rest "D’O classics."
  • •Wine list deliberately small (180 bottles vs. ~310 at a typical one-star) at a low markup (20% vs. ~70%), with 25% of wine revenue from by-the-glass pours (vs. 5% typical) — sells far more bottles/week (205 vs. 75) at a much lower average price (€22 vs. €45).
  • •15–20 local suppliers, dealt with directly; daily fresh sourcing; no exotic/status ingredients (no caviar, no lobster); obsessively documented edible-portion percentages per ingredient to minimize waste.
  • •Staff grew from 4 to 14 (incl. Oldani): head chef, 2 sous-chefs, 3 chefs de partie, 3 commis, 3 dishwashers, 1 sommelier — no professional waiters; chefs rotate serving tables weekly, building a direct relationship with diners.
  • •Work starts 9am (not 6am like top Paris/London kitchens), kitchen closes ~10:30pm, staff get 2 days off/week + 5 weeks off in summer. Only 3 people have left since opening. Recruitment: 8 interviews total, including meeting candidates' parents, plus reading one of Oldani's books before a trial lunch.
  • •Oldani's own tableware designs (Assiette D’O Land plate, Verre D’O glass, Passpartout hybrid fork/knife/spoon) cut breakage, cleaning time, water and soap costs, and reduce table clutter.

Industry & competitive context

  • •Global restaurant industry: €1.2 trillion revenue (2010). Italy alone: 157,000 restaurants, €37.6 billion revenue.
  • •Michelin Guide: launched 1900 (France), starred restaurants since 1926; only ~2,000 restaurants worldwide have ever held a star. 2012 counts: France 594 total (26 three-star), Italy 295 (7 three-star, up from 90 total in 1956), Japan 317, Germany 249, UK 145, US 130.
  • •Typical Michelin restaurant: ~50% capacity utilization; labor = 40–50% of costs; losing a star can cut sales by ~50%.
  • •Setting up a 60-seat restaurant in Italy: €80,000–130,000 up-front.
  • •Failed/partial comparables Oldani was aware of: Mark Veyrat's Cozna Vera (organic-burger chain, opened France 2008, closed 2010); Claudio Sadler's "Chic’n Quick" (a cheaper sibling next to his own two-star restaurant, €19 lunch menu, still running).
The decision on the table
Oldani is deciding how to "spread the POP concept," with two options in play. (A) Clone D’O in a new city (New York, Dubai, London, Tokyo, Singapore, Beijing all floated by interested investors). (B) Open "POP by D’O": a newly available 900 sqm property in Cornaredo (4× D’O's size, ~60 seats), casual communal round tables (6–8 seats), prices 10–15% below D’O, tapas-style small plates, cast-iron hot-plate cooking (cheaper, less water/electricity/detergent), ~€500,000 investment, a wider dinner window (7pm–midnight) with likely more than one table turn. Oldani frames it via an Armani analogy: D’O as the couture line, POP as the accessible diffusion brand. The case ends undecided — cannibalization and brand-confusion risk vs. the appeal of a validated, low-risk-feeling expansion right next door.

  • Q2 — How successful is D’O, really — what does its profitability actually look like?
  • Q3 — What should Oldani do next, and how would he implement it?
Resolved in class

The full VCAP-framework answer to these three questions is worked through in Lecture 1 → Applying it to Ristorante D’O: value proposition (Michelin quality at trattoria prices), competencies (cost control + consistent quality, not variety), assets (small team, durable tableware), and processes (fixed slots, cash-only, chef rotation) that together explain the 100% vs. 50% utilization gap. Case file: ScharffenBerger Chocolate Maker (A) HBS 606-043 · set May 2005

The scene
May 2005. COO Jim Harris steps out of the factory floor in Berkeley, California, as his team preps for a new custom ball mill. Demand has outstripped supply for all 18 months he's been at the company; a mass-market retailer just offered a deal that alone could grow demand 30% by year-end, and his marketing manager thinks demand could triple within a few years.

Company background

  • •Founded 1996 by Robert Steinberg (a family physician who, after a bout with cancer, sold his practice to pursue chocolate, training in France) and John Scharffenberger (had just sold his successful winery).
  • •First small batches 1997, out of rented space in South San Francisco. Moved into a 27,000-sq-ft Berkeley warehouse in May 2001, expecting a fivefold production increase.
  • •Jim Harris recruited end of 2003 from Kohlberg & Company (a local private equity firm) to lead the business and manage growth.
  • •As of May 2005: 60 employees — 24 in production, 18 across five retail stores (Berkeley, San Francisco, Healdsburg CA, and two in New York), 18 in admin/management.
  • •One of the few U.S. makers doing true "bean to bar" production in-house, rather than buying pre-mixed chocolate liquor from large manufacturers like most competitors.

Market & product line

  • •Mass market: $14B, ~75%+ controlled by Hershey, Mars, and Nestlé; flat growth (1–2%/yr); milk chocolate ~70% of U.S. sales (down from ~90% in the 1980s).
  • •Premium segment: $1.2B, retail priced $10–$100/lb, projected 15–20%/yr growth 2000–2010, driven by dark chocolate's health halo (antioxidants) and rising retail availability. Scharffen Berger sat at the high end of price and positioning within premium.
  • •Chief taste competitor: European premium makers like Valrhona; also competed more broadly with Godiva and Ghirardelli/Lindt.
  • •Revenue: $1.1M (1999) → $10.0M (2004) → est. >$15M (2005).
  • •Product line (by cacao %): unsweetened 99%, extra dark 82%, bittersweet 70% (most popular single product, the 3oz bar), semi-sweet/mocha/mint/nibby all 62%, milk chocolate 41%. ~80 total SKUs across sizes (5g/1oz/3oz/10oz consumer; 2kg/3kg/15kg food-service). Unsweetened + extra dark ≈ 20% of sales; sweeter products the other 80%.
  • •Trader Joe's: largest customer, sells an exclusive 60% cacao 2oz bar under the Scharffen Berger brand name (TJ's insisted on keeping the name given its brand equity).

The production process & the ball mill decision

Full quantitative treatment — capacity by stage, the bottleneck, the ball mill's financial case, and the 50/100/150% growth plan — lives in the dedicated Assignment section above. The cold-call facts worth having loose in memory:

  • •8-step process: Bean Cleaner → Roaster → Winnower → Melangeur → Conche (×2) → Tempering → Molding → Packaging.
  • •Conching is the long pole: 40–60 hours per 1,400 kg batch, conches staffed 24/7. Everything else turns over in under 1.25 hours or runs continuously.
  • •Ball mill: $300,000 custom Dutch-built machine. Cuts the combined conche + refine time to ~15 hours (10hr conche + 5hr ball mill), instead of 40–60 hours in the conche alone. Confirmed via four rounds of blind taste tests — no quality loss, possibly slightly improved texture/flavor.
  • •Team's own internal analysis flagged the melangeur as the likely next bottleneck once the ball mill is in; a used replacement melangeur was already located for ~$50,000.
  • •Each completed 1,400kg conche batch worth >$30,000 in sales; industry-average contribution margin ~40%.
  • •Current max monthly capacity ≈40,000 kg (~29 conches); when Harris arrived capacity was only ~18 conches/month at 1 shift, 5 days — raised to 26–29 purely through added shifts, no new equipment.
Resolved in class / in the assignment

Bottleneck (conche, 29.3 batches/month), the ball mill's payback (≈1.4 months), the new bottleneck after installation (melangeur), and the full 50/100/150% shift-and-capex plan are all worked in detail in the Assignment section, including the flow-unit derivation the instructor specifically requires you to justify rather than assume.

The scene
Christy Connor, newly hired Director of North America Sales Support at Baria Planning Solutions (BPS), reads a 4:23am email from her boss Brandon Ali, President of North American Sales: 2010's win rate came in at 15.5% against a 17.5% forecast, and renewal rates slipped from ~91–92% historically to 84%. He wants a proposal, quantifying staffing/utilization/cost, by next week.

Company & industry background

  • •BPS: publicly traded, $95M annual sales, founded 1997. Does "spend analysis" — software + data analysis + project management + consulting to cut clients' procurement costs and improve supplier performance. Held ~18% market share by 2010 in a market now crowded by SAP and Accenture.
  • •Grew via acquisition: government-sector firm (2007), then a manufacturing-focused firm and a retail-focused firm (2008) — each with different tech platforms, processes, even fiscal year-ends. Integration mostly done by end of 2009.
  • •North American Sales has three groups: Sales (Chuck Dee; 4 industry units — Energy, Government, Manufacturing, Retail & Other), Sales Operations (Jane Albright; competitive intel, win-loss analysis, training, staffing, comp plans), and Sales Support (Connor's new team).
  • •Sales Support runs a hybrid structure: Data Engineering, Data Analysis, and Pricing are shared across all industries; only Proposal Support is dedicated by industry. Dee wants the whole team industry-dedicated (he blames shared-team "lack of ownership" for missed deadlines); Albright designed the hybrid model specifically to balance staffing efficiency against industry expertise, and successfully defended it before.
  • •Cross-training was tried and largely failed: Data Engineers ↔ Data Analysts can cross-train (cost ≈1 month salary/benefits per person); Proposal Support can cross-train across industries (cost ≈3 weeks salary/benefits, but Dee opposes it); Pricing staff can't be effectively cross-trained at all. Cross-trained staff also need ≥3 months in a role to become productive.

The solution-selling process & the four sales-support functions

  • •Four opportunity types: New Sale (new customer), Renewal (existing customer, contract renewal), Expansion (more services to an existing customer, off-cycle), Pilot (short trial engagement, often feeds the New Sale pipeline).
  • •Four sequential support functions per opportunity: 1. Data Engineering (collect & clean prospect data) → 2. Data Analysis (run BPS software, find savings opportunities) → 3. Proposal Support (validate/quantify opportunities, build the proposal, help pitch) → 4. Pricing (build the pricing proposal, incl. risk-sharing options).
  • •Renewals specifically: planning starts ~5 weeks before the renewal date, presentation ~2–3 weeks after that, leaving 2–3 weeks for analysis. Mean process times for a renewal: 8 / 12 / 22 / 4 hours across the four functions — seemingly ample time on paper.
  • •Company risk rule of thumb for staff utilization: <70% = low risk, 70–85% = medium risk, 85–100% = high risk, >100% = already over capacity.

The decision on the table

Case file: Rubicon Global HBS 816-015 · set late Nov 2014

Not yet covered in class
Queued for Lecture 12 (final session), explicitly as a VCAP application case — the same framework built in Lecture 1 and applied to D’O. No in-class resolution exists yet.
The scene
Co-founders Nate Morris and Lane Moore walk into a board meeting hoping to close terms on a new financing round (targeted for early 2015). Rubicon has raised roughly $5M+ since 2008, validated its model, and now needs to decide how much to raise, from whom, on what terms — to chase a mission of transforming the $64B U.S. waste industry.

Founder & company background

  • •Nate Morris: born 1980, Lexington, Kentucky. Politically ambitious from a young age (grandfather was a union leader); George Washington University (Scottish Rite Scholar), congressional internships, worked in the Bush White House, fundraising role on Bush's 2004 re-election campaign; later a Woodrow Wilson School graduate program at Princeton.
  • •2008: introduced by high-school friend Marc Spiegel (family in the garbage-hauling trade for a century) to the Oakleaf business model — an asset-light waste broker with 2,500+ haulers, sold for $655M to New Mountain Capital (having raised $30M from Charterhouse Capital in 2003). Morris and Spiegel believed Oakleaf's model was inefficient because it lacked a technology platform.
  • •Rubicon launched 2008; Morris maxed out personal credit cards for the initial website and legal fees. First major customer: a national pizza chain, landed via a Kentucky real-estate-broker connection (Henry "Hank" Dudgeon III, who then joined as co-founder/Director of Business Development). Pitch: simplified contract management/invoicing, transparency into waste destination, and creative reuse (e.g., selling waste pizza dough to ethanol producers).
  • •Raised $1M from Lane Moore's QuarterMoore Capital to build out software; Moore had earlier founded and sold "Bagster" (a "dumpster in a bag" product) to Waste Management in 2009.
  • •February 2012: $5M Series-A led by Peter Kellner (Richmond Global), with strategic investors including Bill Gatton, Rotunda Capital Partners, and the Brown-Forman family office (Jack Daniels/Korbel).
  • •By spring 2014: 22,000 customer locations serviced through a network of 10,000 haulers/processors (up from 7,000 locations three years earlier, without materially growing headcount). Nate Morris named to Fortune's 2014 "40 Under 40." Oscar Salazar (Uber co-founder) reportedly told Morris "Rubicon could be bigger than Uber."
  • •Rubicon completed B Corporation certification in 2012 (B Lab had certified 950 companies across 30+ countries by 2014, including Patagonia, Seventh Generation, Sungevity).

Business model

  • •Positioned as "an Uber for trash" (New York Times) — a cloud-based, asset-light, multi-sided platform connecting four groups: customers who need waste removed, recyclers, independent haulers, and landfills. Rubicon owns no trucks, no landfills.
  • •Contrasts directly with incumbents Waste Management (NYSE: WM) and Republic Services (NYSE: RSG), who own trucks and landfills and profit from maximizing landfill tonnage (landfill/transfer EBIT was >30% of total EBIT at major players; WM's minimum required IRR was 12% for collection/landfill projects vs. 15% for recycling — incentives literally favor landfilling over recycling).
  • •Three revenue streams: a fee based on waste composition/volume, a software/management fee (invoicing, reporting), and a diversion bonus tied to tonnage kept out of landfills. Contracts typically 2–3 years.

Step 0: choosing and justifying the flow unit Case, Exhibits 4–5

Answer
The bottleneck is the conche stage: 29.3 batches/month ≈ 41,000 kg/month, matching the case's own reported current capacity (“≈40,000 kg, 29 conches”) almost exactly.
Why here, not elsewhere
Every other stage runs at 8–16 hr/day; conches run 24/7 and still take 40–60 hours per 1,400 kg batch — a dwell time two to three orders of magnitude longer than any other step.
Second-tightest stage
The roaster (77.5% utilized) is the closest runner-up, purely because it's still parked at 1 shift/day while melangeur, tempering and molding already run 2.
Operational implications — Q1
Conching is the bottleneck partly by physics (long batch dwell time) and partly by product mix: the lower-cacao, higher-sugar recipes Harris is chasing for growth (Trader Joe's 60%, milk chocolate 40%) sit at the slow end of the 40–60 hr range. That creates a real strategy-vs-capacity tension the report should name explicitly: leaning further into private-label/mass-market volume (the growth Harris wants) pushes the weighted-average conche time up and the bottleneck's throughput down, at the same time as demand is rising — the two forces compound rather than cancel.

Any new or used unit should go through the same taste-test protocol used to qualify the ball mill before it's trusted with full production volume — skipping that step to chase capacity would repeat the exact mistake Harris was careful to avoid with the ball mill itself.

Little’s Law Slides 5, 9, 11

Inventory, I
Average number of flow units in the process at any time.
Throughput, R
Average number of flow units exiting per unit time.
Flow time, T
Average time a flow unit spends in the process.
I = R × T

Rate × time = distance. Worked from the Presbyterian Hospital radiology log Slides 6–10: 11 patients over an 11-hour day → throughput = 1 patient/hr; total flow time 22.83 hrs → average flow time 2.076 hrs; and by Little's Law average inventory = 1 × 2.076 = 2.076 patients — the same number arrived at either from raw timestamps or from the rate/time relationship.

“Buy 2, get 1 free”: know any two of I, R, T and the third is determined. And it holds always — independent of queue discipline (FCFS, LIFO, random) and even under randomness in arrivals/processing (the subject of Lectures 4–6). Discipline changes an individual customer's flow time and the inventory mix, never the throughput rate. Slide 11

Causes of distress: first- vs. second-order variability Slides 26–29

First-order variability
Knowable changes in the mean of demand or supply — e.g., mean pumpkin demand predictably rises near Halloween. Amazon's and Walmart's revenue both show strong, predictable quarterly seasonality.
Second-order variability
Unknowable randomness around that mean — the exact daily pumpkin demand, or year-to-year crop yield. Covered starting Lecture 4.

First-order first: both types exist simultaneously and both cause distress even when perfectly known in advance, but you must address the predictable shift in the mean before spending effort on the unpredictable noise around it. Slide 29

What happens when average demand exceeds average capacity Slide 31

Short term
Customers wait (queue grows), are blocked (can't join the queue), balk (decline to join), or defect/renege (leave before being served).
Long term
Management invests to expand capacity, customers permanently move to competitors, or quality degrades — each opening a market opportunity for someone else.

Matching capacity to seasonal demand Slides 30, 34–35

Chase strategy
Set capacity (regular + overtime + subcontract) to the peak rate; produce to order. No inventory risk, but pay for idle peak capacity in the off-season.
Level strategy
Set capacity at the average demand rate; build inventory in the low season to sell in the high season (assumes regular capacity alone can cover the average).
Hybrid strategy
Build some inventory ahead of peak, then produce-to-order for the remainder of the peak — the realistic middle ground most firms actually run, trading inventory carrying cost against capacity investment.

Measuring variability: the coefficient of variation Slides 12-17

Variability comes from two sides, and each gets its own CV (standard deviation ÷ mean, a unit-free number):

Demand (arrival) variability
CVa = σa ÷ a, where σa is the standard deviation of the time between arrivals and a is the average interarrival time. Arrival rate λ = 1/a.
Supply (service) variability
CVp = σp ÷ p, where σp is the standard deviation of the time to serve and p is the average service time. Capacity rate μ = 1/p.
Service CVp Slide 13
  • Below 1: uniform, repetitive work (Jiffy Lube, Shouldice Hospital, simple wills)
  • About 1: customer-specific jobs (custom design, car dealer service, general surgery, family law)
  • Above 1: jobs with several parts done at once (omni-channel marketing, auto restoration, emergency surgery, corporate law)
Arrival CVa Slide 14
  • Equal to 1: independent arrivals, e.g. walk-ins. This is what “random” means
  • Below 1: arrivals follow a schedule, e.g. doctor’s appointments

Throughput, utilization, and safety capacity Slides 17-19

Throughput rate
min{λ, μ}. Stable queueing models need λ < μ. Example: λ = 10/hr, μ = 12/hr gives throughput 10/hr, assuming nobody is blocked, balks, or defects. Otherwise throughput is lower.
Utilization, ρ
ρ = throughput ÷ capacity = λ ÷ (m×μ) = p ÷ (m×a), with m = number of servers. It is the % of time each server is working.
Worked example
10 customers/hr, 3 servers at 4/hr each: ρ = 10 ÷ (3×4) = 10/12 = 83.3%.
Safety capacity
100% − utilization = 16.7% in the example above. The slack that absorbs variability.

The system-time formula Slide 20

The slide gives the approximation the rest of the unit runs on. Average system time is wait plus service, and the wait is the product of three effects:

Wait = (p/m) × [(CVa² + CVp²) / 2] × [ρ√(2(m+1)) − 1 / (1 − ρ)] System time = Wait + p. The three terms: service time per server, variation effect, dependency effect.
Service time per server, p/m
More servers shorten it, and so do faster ones.
Variation effect
The average of the squared arrival and service CVs. Only this combined number matters, not which side is the variable one.
Dependency effect
Probability a customer waits ÷ safety capacity. It explodes as utilization nears 100%.

Lecture 1: Operations strategy and VCAP

All MCQ material. No calculation, but the D’O case facts are fair game.

V = C × (A + P)  ·  Operations strategy = the optimal configuration of assets and processes that supports the business strategy V = value (a vector: price, quality, timeliness, variety). C = competencies. A = assets. P = processes.
[EXAM] Value to competency to operations focus
  • Price → cost efficiency. Quality → quality processes and assets. Timeliness → responsiveness (shorter response time). Variety → flexibility.
  • Price (what the customer sees) is not cost (internal). Keep them apart.
  • Value is a vector, not a single number. Alignment is the success principle: design operations from what the firm promised customers, in a back-and-forth with business strategy.
  • Walmart: everyday low price, with selection and inventory good enough, so processes and assets target cost.
Assets and processes trade off
  • Strong assets allow weaker processes, and strong processes compensate for weak assets. Uber is asset-light and process-strong. Waste collection is asset-heavy with high fixed cost.
  • Competency comes from the synergy between assets and processes, which is what competitors cannot copy. Anyone can buy the same machine.
  • Three levels of fit: each choice reinforces the operations strategy, assets and processes have synergy within a business line, and operations is coordinated with marketing and finance (unaligned marketing leaves assets idle or demand unserved).
  • A firm can have several business lines, each with its own operations strategy under one corporate strategy.
Asset decisions: location, size, type, timing
  • Location: mostly fixed cost. Downtown rent flows into price. D’O sits about 35 km from central Milan for exactly this reason.
  • Size: capacity and safety capacity. At 100% utilization there is no safety capacity. Right-size, not maximize.
  • Type: dedicated vs flexible vs partially flexible. Flexibility = one resource performs more than one task. A little flexibility goes a long way, with diminishing returns. We right-size flexibility, we do not maximize it.
  • Timing: capacity leading (anticipate demand), lagging (wait until demand is sticky, as ScharffenBerger and D’O did), or tracking. Leading earns market position and costs opportunity cost if demand does not come.
Process decisions: supply, demand, risk, technology, innovation
  • Supply: one vs many suppliers, in-house vs outsource, volume, supplier participation in design, yield and loss pricing, service guarantees.
  • Demand: which demand to accept (batch sizes, order caps) because setup time kills productivity. Inventory build-up suits regular products, not customized ones.
  • Risk = second-order variability. Managed with safety capacity, safety inventory, and safety time.
  • Technology: tech vs touch. D’O is cash-only because card fees of 3 to 6% conflict with a price-based proposition.
  • Innovation: how often you iterate. More variety needs more flexibility, which operations dislikes. Toyota and BMW can differ inside one sector.
[EXAM] Efficient frontier and productivity
  • Axes: cost on x, a non-cost competency (quality, variety, timeliness) on y. Firms on the frontier must trade off: gain something, lose something (higher quality costs productivity). Firms inside the frontier can improve without a trade-off.
  • The frontier moves only with innovation in business and operations strategy. D’O pushed it out with a new business model. Resource pooling (Lecture 5) is also an operations innovation that improves position with no trade-off.
  • Productivity (output over input) cannot compare firms with different strategic positions. Toyota builds about 300,000 cars a year and Rolls-Royce a few thousand. Rolls-Royce is not “a bad company,” it sells exclusivity, not low price.
D’O case facts to hold on to
  • Value proposition: excellent food made accessible (price-led), so every operating choice is judged against it.
  • Choose the change with the highest ROIC that does not violate the proposition. Raising price or the wine markup lifts ROIC but conflicts with the proposition. Dropping the 8:30 time slot lifts return to about 18% and is aligned. ROIC for the case was about 7.7% on operating capital.
  • The long reservation wait list works as a buffer against demand variability and lets the firm focus on cost. Removing flexibility raised throughput but needed more staff, which raised price. That broke alignment.
  • Investigate outliers, do not just delete them.
Exam-ready takeaway: for any Lecture 1 MCQ, name the value proposition first, then ask which competency it needs, then check that the asset or process choice supports that competency without breaking the proposition.

Lecture 2: Process capacity analysis

20 pts numerical plus MCQs. Work in rates, apply the minimum rule, keep units consistent.

μ = 1/p  ·  capacity = m × μ  ·  process capacity = min over stations  ·  R = min(demand, capacity) Utilization = R / capacity of that resource. Implied utilization = demand / capacity (no min, can exceed 100%). Tmin = sum of p along one path.
[EXAM] Capacity and the bottleneck
  • p = 1/μ. Put p in the same unit as the rate you want: 10 min = 1/6 hour, so μ = 6 per hour. [TRAP] Most students lose marks on this conversion.
  • m identical parallel resources: station capacity = m × μ. Parallel resources each take whole flow units, they do not cooperate.
  • Bottleneck = the station with the lowest long-term capacity, not the slowest processing time. A slow p is compensated by more resources. Memorize: (1) bottleneck = lowest long-term capacity, (2) bottleneck capacity = process capacity.
  • “The minimum rule is your friend in the exam.” Always work with capacity rates once any station has more than one resource.
  • Why long-term: in the short run capacity can catch up in later periods. In the long run a persistent shortfall makes the backlog explode.
[EXAM] Throughput, utilization, implied utilization
  • Throughput (flow, exit, output rate) R = min(demand rate, process capacity). Count units leaving, not arriving. “Service rate” is not a synonym.
  • Utilization = R / resource capacity, never above 100%. Write it this way and you cannot get any station wrong.
  • Process utilization = the bottleneck’s utilization. Do not report a resource-level figure like 57% as the process utilization.
  • Implied utilization = demand / capacity. 133% means a 33% capacity deficit. 200% means double the capacity. Equals utilization when demand is below capacity.
  • The highest-utilization resource is the bottleneck and the first one to bind as demand grows. Longest flow time is not the criterion.
  • Deterministic setting: unused capacity is underutilization (demand-constrained), not safety capacity. Real systems keep slack because of variability.
[EXAM] Tmin and flow time
  • Tmin = sum of the processing times along one path through an empty system. Minimum because no one waits, theoretical because the system is deterministic. Actual flow time is Tmin or worse.
  • Capacity is unrelated to Tmin. Process capacity is not 1/Tmin, because Tmin ignores how many resources a station has.
  • Adding capacity to a non-bottleneck, even infinite, changes neither process capacity nor flow time.
  • Shortening a non-bottleneck still has value (customer experience, lower WIP, cost). “Offers no operational benefit” is false.
Flow unit, yield, flow diagram
  • Define the process boundary first, then pick one flow unit and convert every station into it. The unit changes as it moves (beans, nibs, paste, chocolate), so convert with the actual input to each station.
  • Loss at a stage: multiply the yields (95% then 95% gives 90.25%). Work backwards for required input.
  • Flow diagram: boxes for activities, triangles for waiting. Draw a buffer before the bottleneck. For process-analysis questions no buffers are needed in the deterministic setting. A cooling step that needs attention is an activity, a pure wait is a triangle.
  • Moves between stations with no waiting are added to the neighbouring activity time.
ScharffenBerger, the one case question
  • Why the ball mill does not add 75%: conching capacity jumps from about 40k to 68k kg a month, but process capacity only rises to the next limit (the roaster, about +10%). Fix the bottleneck and another resource becomes the bottleneck. The gain depends on the next constraint.
  • Typical OM pitfall: improving one part and forgetting the minimum rule. Check for a second bottleneck every time.
  • Growth: +50% demand needs a second roaster shift, +100% needs more shifts at several stations, +150% leaves everything near capacity, so any variability breaks the process. Operational choices must still protect quality, the value proposition.
Multiple flow-unit types (likely not tested, cheap to know)
Weighted-average time per task over the customer mix (a synthetic flow unit), add every task the same resource performs (a resource seen twice counts both times), convert to a rate, multiply by availability, take the minimum across resources. Valley Forge tax-firm example: admin 71.5 min per client gives 134 clients a month at 160 hours.
Exam-ready takeaway: six steps every time: define the flow unit, compute m×μ per station in one time unit, take the minimum, set R = min(demand, capacity), compute utilization per resource and implied utilization, then Tmin as the sum of p. State units on every line.

Lecture 3: Little’s Law and first-order variability

One MCQ-style question on the financial version, and the 20-point production-strategy question.

I = R × T  ·  cash-to-cash = cost-to-cash − AP flow time Any two of I, R, T give the third. Units must match.
[EXAM] Little’s Law
  • I = R × T: average inventory = throughput rate × average flow time. Holds for any boundary and any queue discipline (FCFS, LIFO, SPT) as long as customers are patient.
  • [TRAP] Match the units. 10,000 claims a year over 50 weeks is 200 a week, times 3 weeks gives 600 claims. One coffee every 30 seconds is 2 a minute, so 6 in the system means 3 minutes.
  • [TRAP] Name the flow unit (the claim, the bottle, the pound, the dollar), not the customer.
  • Snapshot reading: at any moment you see about I units in the system. Fractions are fine (2.08 patients). Do not round down.
  • Inflow/outflow diagram: vertical gap = inventory, horizontal gap = flow time (FCFS). Average inventory, throughput, and flow time all change with the window you analyse, and the law holds in each.
  • Inside a queue: T = Tq + p, Iq = λTq, number in service = λp = utilization (times m for m servers). Whole system: I = λT.
  • Turns: turns = R / I = 1 / flow time. 104 turns a year means a 0.5 week flow time. Inventory and payables relate to COGS, receivables to sales.
[EXAM] Cash cycles (he expects one question)
  • Inventory flow time = 365 × inventory / COGS. Payables flow time = 365 × AP / COGS. Receivables flow time = 365 × AR / sales.
  • Cost-to-cash = inventory days + receivable days. Cash-to-cash = cost-to-cash − payable days. The slide label “flow time of inventory” on the AP bar should read accounts payable, which the professor corrected himself.
  • Worked check (sales $250M, COGS $176M, AR $28M, inventory $50.6M, AP $11.9M): payables 24.7 days, inventory 104.9, receivables 40.9. Cost-to-cash about 146 days, cash-to-cash about 121 days. (He once says “101”: a slip, the arithmetic gives 121.)
  • The cycle can be negative: suppliers and customers finance you, which signals bargaining power or a very fast process. Levers: longer supplier terms, faster internal conversion, faster collection.
  • A supplier discount lowers payables and COGS together, so the effect largely cancels. Do the math, do not assume.
[EXAM] First vs second order
  • First-order variability = predictable change in the average (seasonality). Second-order = unpredictable noise around the average. Both can occur at once, on supply and on demand.
  • Priority: first make sure average capacity covers average demand. Then absorb the noise with safety capacity, safety inventory, or safety time.
  • OM triangle: capacity, inventory, information. Inventory is the physical form of missing information. With no information you buy capacity or inventory.
  • Balking = refuse to join after seeing the line. Reneging = join, then leave. Blocking = no room. Capacity below demand in the long run is not sustainable.
  • Even with daily capacity above daily demand, a temporary surge builds a queue.
[EXAM] Chase, level, hybrid
  • Chase: capacity follows demand each season. No inventory, but needs flexible capacity.
  • Level: capacity = average demand all year. Build inventory in the low season, run it down in the peak. Cost is holding cost only, the area under the inventory curve times the carrying cost per unit.
  • Hybrid: capacity between average and peak demand. Smaller shortfall, so it starts building later, holds less inventory, but pays for more capacity.
  • Level builds earlier at a lower rate, so its inventory triangle is larger than the hybrid’s. Which is cheaper depends on the cost of extra capacity against the cost of holding inventory.
[EXAM] The recipe for the 20-point question (Lecture 3 slides 32-37)
  1. Magnitude of first-order variability = peak demand − low demand.
  2. Shortfall per week = peak demand − capacity. Total shortage = shortfall per week × weeks of peak. This equals the maximum inventory.
  3. Build rate = capacity − low-season demand.
  4. Weeks of build = total shortage / build rate. Start that many weeks before the peak begins.
  5. Inventory cost = area under the inventory curve (triangles) × cost per unit per week.

His example: capacity 100, peak demand 120 for 12 weeks, low demand 40, peak starts at the beginning of week 41. Shortage 20 × 12 = 240. Build rate 100 − 40 = 60. Build time 240/60 = 4 weeks, so production for stock starts at the beginning of week 37, with 240 units on hand when the peak starts. “Don’t forget the system runs 40 full weeks at low demand.” Full numerical comparison with level and chase is in the walkthroughs below.

Exam-ready takeaway: draw the inventory triangle before computing. Slope up = capacity minus low demand, slope down = peak demand minus capacity, height = total shortage. Everything else is reading that picture.

Lecture 4: Second-order variability

The professor says Lectures 4 and 5 are where students struggle. Master the definitions and the CV interpretations.

a = 1/λ  ·  CVa = σa/a  ·  CVp = σp/p  ·  ρ = λ/(mμ) = λp/m Standing assumption in Lectures 4 and 5: λ < mμ, so throughput = λ and customers are patient.
[EXAM] Notation and measurement
  • a = average inter-arrival time (average the gaps between consecutive arrival times). σa = its standard deviation. If a = 1/10 hour, λ = 10 per hour.
  • p = average processing time, σp its standard deviation, μ = 1/p.
  • Standard deviation is never negative. Averages are held fixed, only the spread varies.
  • Core intuition: the doctor sees a patient every 5 minutes and takes 4 minutes exactly, so utilization is 80% and there is no queue. Same averages with random timing and queues form. Congestion comes from variability, not from lost capacity. Do not say “not enough capacity” when 60 tourists arrive as one batch.
  • Implied utilization example: an arrival every 5 minutes against 10 minutes of service is 12 arrivals an hour against 6 capacity, 200%. Unsustainable.
[EXAM] Reading CVa (likely MCQ)
  • CVa = 0: deterministic. Identical gaps. Operations loves it, reality never delivers it.
  • CVa < 1: appointment system. Some spread around the booked time.
  • CVa = 1: purely random, Poisson arrivals, exponential gaps, nobody coordinates (grocery store).
  • CVa > 1: batch arrivals. Tiny gaps inside a batch and huge gaps between batches. Worse than random.
[EXAM] Reading CVp
  • CVp < 1: standardized work, small spread (tire and oil changes, a single-procedure hernia clinic).
  • CVp = 1: random, customer-specific (general surgery, 45 minutes to 7 hours).
  • CVp > 1: individualized, fully customized, heavy tail (emergency department, 2 minutes of resuscitation to 14 hours of operating).
  • CVa describes how customers arrive, CVp how they are processed. Worst case: batch arrivals and fully customized service.
  • Real distributions are usually exponential or hyper-exponential, not normal. Worked CVs: mean 3, sd 1.1 gives 0.36.
[EXAM] Variability and utilization (an old free-response, now an MCQ)
  • ρ = λp/m uses averages only. Variability by itself does not change utilization when customers are patient. 1 min + 9 min of work has the same utilization as 5 min + 5 min.
  • Balking, reneging, or blocking lowers utilization, because effective λ falls and work is lost.
  • Variability changes waiting, not utilization.
  • Averages mislead: an average wait of 30 minutes does not mean everyone waits 30. Some wait 90. This sets up the exponential tail in Lecture 5.
  • An emergency department is not FCFS (triage), a note on why its waits differ from the formula.
Exam-ready takeaway: convert everything to the same time unit first. Then compute CVa = σa/a and CVp = σp/p. If the question says M/M, skip both, they equal 1. Deterministic arrivals mean CVa = 0.

Lecture 5: Queueing models and timeliness

Three formulas, no more. Write the expanded wait formula on your aid sheet.

Tq = (p/m) × [(CVa² + CVp²)/2] × [ρ√(2(m+1)) − 1 / (1 − ρ)] T = Tq + p  ·  Iq = λTq  ·  number in service = ρm  ·  I = Iq + ρm = λT  ·  P(wait > t) = e−t/Tq
[EXAM] The three effects
  • Service effect p/m: more or faster servers cut it.
  • Variation effect (CVa² + CVp²)/2: a simple average, not weighted. Arrival CV 0.5 with service CV 1 gives the same wait as arrival CV 1 with service CV 0.5.
  • Dependency effect ρ√(2(m+1)) − 1/(1 − ρ): the numerator is P(wait), the denominator is safety capacity. For m = 1 the exponent is 1, so P(wait) = ρ.
  • The three multiply. If any one worsens, the wait worsens.
  • M/M means CVa = CVp = 1, so the variation effect is 1 and drops out. G/G means compute both CVs.
  • Formulas are approximations. QMacros differs slightly for G/G, with the same insight. QMacros is not on the exam.
[EXAM] Comparative statics: add one server (m to m+1)
  • p/m falls, ρ falls, P(wait) falls (a number between 0 and 1 raised to a bigger power shrinks), safety capacity rises.
  • CVa and CVp are unchanged. Nothing in the variation term depends on m.
  • New technology that cuts p: capacity up and ρ down, but σp may not shrink in proportion, so CVp could rise. If no new σp is given, assume CVp is unchanged.
  • In the exam formula, adding servers with everything else fixed always helps. Lecture 6 specialization logic does not apply.
[EXAM] Utilization and safety capacity
  • Safety capacity = 1 − ρ. It can be tiny but never zero, because it is in the denominator.
  • Safety capacity = 1 − P(wait) only when m = 1.
  • Nonlinear near the top: ρ/(1 − ρ) is 1 at 50%, 1.04 at 51%, 49 at 98%, 99 at 99%. A one-point rise at 98% doubles the dependency factor.
  • Never say “maximize utilization.” Talk in utilization and safety capacity. The right level depends on the stakes: an ambulance is not a coffee shop.
  • More variability needs more safety capacity for the same wait. With no variability you can run near 100%. “If you want to get rid of wait time, get rid of variability.”
  • Waiting only happens when all servers are busy at once. With m servers, P(wait) is below ρ.
[EXAM] Pooling
  • Call centre, same λ = 100 per hour, same total capacity 120: one fast server (μ = 120) gives Tq 1.56 and T 2.06 min. Ten slow servers (μ = 12 each) give P(wait) 51%, Tq 0.96, but T 5.96 min because service takes 5 minutes. Pooling lowers the wait and can raise total time. Look at T, not only Tq.
  • One shared line vs separate lines (coffee shop, λ = 95 per hour, two baristas at 50 per hour, both at ρ = 95%): separate lines each have Tq 22.8 and T 24 min. A shared two-server line has T about 12.3 min. Same utilization, lower P(wait), balanced workload.
  • Costs: customers prefer to be last in a short line, a long single queue can drive balking, and servers must be interchangeable. Which design wins depends on whether customers react to the wait or to total time.
[EXAM] Long waits, the exponential tail
  • P(wait ≤ t) = 1 − e−t/Tq and P(wait > t) = e−t/Tq. Valid when arrivals and service are random (M/M). Strict vs non-strict inequality makes no difference for a continuous wait.
  • Example: Tq = 33.75, t = 60. 1 − e−1.778 = 0.831, so 83% wait under an hour and 17% wait longer. The professor expects you to compute Tq first, then plug it in, and T is given.
  • Expected question shape: compute CVa, CVp and ρ, then Tq, then T, Iq, I, then the tail probability. Check that I − Iq equals ρm.
  • Clinic exam-style example from the review: λ = 10.8 per hour, μ = 12 per hour (p = 5 min), ρ = 0.9, CVp = 0.7. Tq about 33 min, T about 38, Iq about 5.9, I about 6.8. He called it “a good example we can use for the exam.”
Exam-ready takeaway: compute in this order: λ and p in one unit, ρ, CVs, Tq, then T = Tq + p, then Little’s Law, then the tail. For any MCQ, say which of the three effects moves and in which direction, then read the answer off the formula.

1The bottleneck is the station with the lowest processing time.L2
False. The bottleneck is the station with the lowest capacity m×μ. A station with several resources can have a long p and still not bind.
2Process capacity is 1/Tmin.L2
False. Tmin adds up processing times and ignores the number of resources. Capacity does not depend on it.
3Doubling the capacity of a non-bottleneck station raises process capacity and shortens flow time.L2
False. Neither changes. The minimum rule still points at the old bottleneck.
4Shortening the processing time at a non-bottleneck offers no operational benefit.L2
False. Capacity does not move, but flow time, WIP, and customer experience can improve.
5Implied utilization can exceed 100%, utilization cannot.L2
True. Implied utilization = demand/capacity with no min. Utilization uses throughput, which is capped by capacity.
6If a question asks for the utilization of the process, report the utilization of the resource with the most units.L2
False. Process utilization is the bottleneck’s utilization (100% when demand exceeds capacity). Resource-level figures like 57% describe individual resources.
7Throughput rate and service rate are synonyms.L2
False. Throughput = exit = output = flow rate. Service rate is a capacity concept.
8After ScharffenBerger installs the ball mill, capacity rises by the full improvement at the ball mill.L2 case
False. Another resource (the roaster) becomes the bottleneck, so the gain is limited to the next constraint (about +10%, not +75%). Minimum rule.
9Process-analysis questions require buffers (triangles) in the flow diagram for deterministic processes.L2
False. No buffers are needed in the deterministic setting. Buffers matter once there is variability, and go before the bottleneck.
10Little’s Law only holds for first-come-first-served queues.L3
False. It holds for any discipline with patient customers. Discipline changes who waits, not the average inventory.
11Cash-to-cash can be negative.L3
True. If you collect from customers before you pay suppliers (long payables, short receivables, fast process).
12Cost-to-cash = cash-to-cash + payables flow time.L3
True. Cost-to-cash = inventory days + receivable days. Subtract payable days to get cash-to-cash.
13Seasonal demand is second-order variability.L3
False. Seasonality is first-order (a predictable change in the average). Second-order is unpredictable noise around the average.
14A hybrid strategy holds less inventory than a level strategy but needs more capacity.L3
True. Higher capacity shrinks the peak shortfall, so the build starts later and the inventory triangle is smaller. See the walkthrough: 1,920 vs 19,200 unit-weeks.
15A chase strategy builds inventory in the low season.L3
False. Chase sets capacity equal to demand, so inventory stays near zero. Level and hybrid build stock.
16Balking and reneging are the same thing.L3
False. Balking = refuse to join after seeing the line. Reneging = join then leave, which is worse because the line was inflated and capacity was spent on the customer.
17For a system with deterministic arrivals, CVa = 0.L4
True. Identical gaps mean σa = 0.
18Batch arrivals correspond to CVa > 1; an appointment system to CVa < 1; Poisson arrivals to CVa = 1.L4
True. All three.
19Higher variability increases utilization.L4
False. Utilization depends on averages. Variability lengthens waits. If it causes balking, reneging, or blocking, utilization drops.
20Congestion means the process lacks capacity.L4
False. Queues form with spare capacity whenever arrivals or service times vary. A batch of 60 tourists can congest a system that is fine on average.
21A process with arrival CV 0.5 and service CV 1 has the same expected wait as one with arrival CV 1 and service CV 0.5 (same λ, p, m).L5
True. The variation effect is (CVa² + CVp²)/2, symmetric in the two.
22Safety capacity always equals 1 minus the probability of waiting.L5
False. Only for a single server, where P(wait) = ρ. With m > 1, safety capacity is 1 − ρ and P(wait) is lower than ρ.
23Adding a server reduces the service effect, the probability of waiting, and raises safety capacity, but does not change CVa or CVp.L5
True. All four statements hold inside the exam formula.
24Cutting p with new technology lowers Tq for sure.L5
Not for sure. ρ and p/m fall, but σp may not shrink proportionally, so CVp could rise. If no new σp is given, assume CVp is unchanged, and then yes.
25Splitting one fast server into many slow servers with the same total capacity always reduces the time a customer spends in the system.L5
False. Wait falls (1.56 to 0.96 min in the call centre) but system time rises (2.06 to 5.96 min) because each service takes longer.
26A single shared line with two servers beats two separate lines at the same utilization.L5
True on wait and system time (24 down to about 12.3 min in the coffee shop). It gives up customer continuity and may raise balking if the line looks long.
27If the average wait is 34 minutes, no customer waits more than 60 minutes.L5
False. With exponential waits, about 17% exceed 60 minutes.
28At 98% utilization, one more point of utilization has the same effect on waiting as one more point at 50%.L5
False. The dependency factor ρ/(1−ρ) goes 1 to 1.04 from 50% to 51%, and 49 to 99 from 98% to 99%.
29Utilization should be maximized to use assets well.L1, L5
False. Safety capacity cannot be zero. Choose utilization to match the promised timeliness and the stakes.
30For a firm on the efficient frontier, quality can rise with no loss on another dimension.L1
False. On the frontier you trade off. Only innovation (new business or operations strategy, or pooling) moves the frontier outward. Firms inside it can improve for free.
31Toyota has higher productivity than Rolls-Royce, so Toyota executes its strategy better.L1
False. Productivity measures cost efficiency only, not strategic positioning. Rolls-Royce competes on exclusivity.
32In the VCAP framework, strong assets allow weaker processes and vice versa.L1
True. They partly substitute. Uber (asset-light, process-strong) against a garbage hauler (asset-heavy).

23.4 Western Pennsylvania Milk: stock-out and recoveryVery likelySet 3 · L2-3

● Learning summary Slide 27
  • Dedicated queues beat a pooled system when customers are essentially heterogeneous in average processing time and variability.
  • Sequential queues beat parallel ones when specialization reduces average processing time and the arrival and process variabilities, especially at the first server.
  • Blocking is an effective demand-management tool: reject orders in anticipation of a long wait or because there is no room.
